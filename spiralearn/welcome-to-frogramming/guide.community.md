## A note on the word

The word "community" is doing several different jobs in this curriculum. Before
saying what the course is _for_ communities, it helps to be precise about which
communities, in which sense.

| Sense                       | Who                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | What relationship                                                                                        |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The classroom community** | Alumni, current learners, cohorts                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Co-makers of the curriculum's principles — they were in the room while the principles were being refined |
| **The collegial community** | Friends, mentors, co-developers                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Conversation partners — the syllabus was, and is, a thing being discussed                                |
| **The partnered community** | Cohort hosts, partner organizations                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Settings the curriculum is actually being used in, on real terms with real stakes                        |
| **The discourse community** | The writers and researchers whose work the curriculum draws on (Bruner, Perkins, Wiley, Van Merriënboer & Kirschner, Friston, Malaise & Signer, Cunningham, Wing, Mikhak, Wilson, Sorva, Hofstadter, Karpathy, Willison, and many more) | The long conversation the curriculum is a node in                                                        |

This manifesto addresses **all of these communities at once**. They are not the
same group of people, and the relationships are not the same. But they are
connected by one structural fact: **the course is being made by them and with
them, not just delivered to them.**

That structural fact is the most important thing to know about this curriculum's
community register.

## The course is being made in community

Plain language version:

- The principles were developed across years of teaching, in classrooms with
  real students — including alumni who are now collaborators on the course's
  next versions.
- The framings were sharpened in conversation with friends and colleagues —
  people pushing back, people offering analogies, people flagging when something
  was off.
- The prototype cohorts are not pilots-before-the-real-thing — they _are_ the
  thing. The course shape changes based on what happens in those rooms.
- The platform underneath is being built collaboratively, by a small community
  of practitioners working in the same open-source idiom.
- The intellectual content of the course is itself a synthesis of work the
  author has been reading and being shaped by for years — many of the writers
  will never meet the author; they're still in the room.

There is no version of this course that was first made and then shared. **The
making and the sharing are the same activity, distributed across the communities
listed above.**

## What this means for the role of "the community" in this curriculum

In many curricula, "community" appears as a hosting-context — _where_ the
learning happens, _who_ the learners are, _what_ the social setting provides.
That register is here too: classrooms host learners; cohorts provide peers;
mentors offer guidance.

But the deeper relationship is **co-making**. The community-of-makers and the
community-of-users are not different groups. They are the same kind of
relationship at different moments:

- A learner today is a teacher tomorrow (and is a _companion_ and an
  _accounta-buddy_ by the afternoon)
- A mentor offering guidance this week is taking notes for their own practice
  the following week
- A cohort host hosting a cohort is not running a _delivery_ — they are running
  an iteration that the next version of the course will be shaped by
- An alumni offering feedback on a chapter is contributing the same kind of work
  the author did

This is what _fluid roles_ means, in the operational form the curriculum
inherits from earlier writing (Making Best Practice Common Practice, 2020):

> _"It is useful to think of the roles that need to be supported rather than the
> individuals taking them on:_
>
> - _the learner: this role is taken on when someone's objective is to attain
>   the objectives of the training_
> - _the companion: taken on when someone is helping another to attain a course
>   objective, but has not yet mastered the objective themselves_
> - _the teacher: taken on when someone is helping another to attain a course
>   objective, and has mastered the objective_
> - _the accounta-buddy: taken on when someone is assessing and tracking others'
>   progress."_

A single person, on a single afternoon, will inhabit most or all four of these
roles. The course is designed to support all four on equal footing — same
vocabulary, same framings, same materials. The _difference between_ a teacher
and a learner is the role they're in _right now_, not a permanent identity.

This is what `Wear Hats, not Titles` means, scaled up from individuals to
communities. Roles are situational. Communities are constituted by the roles
their members are in _right now_, not by their static membership lists.

## The course is for communities — but not in a service-provider sense

A clarifying distinction.

A textbook is _for_ a class in a service-provider sense: the textbook exists,
the class buys it, the textbook is consumed. The relationship is one-way.

This curriculum is _for_ communities in a different sense: the curriculum is
partly _constituted by_ what happens in the communities that use it. Cohort
feedback shapes the next version. Partner organizations' constraints become
design considerations. Alumni's discoveries become exercises. The course doesn't
end when a cohort ends; the cohort writes the next iteration into the course's
body.

This means several practical things:

### 1. Forking is welcome

If a community needs to adapt the course — for a different language, a different
learner population, a different cultural context, a different duration — the
curriculum is committed to being **forkable in Wiley's strict sense**. Not just
open-source-licensed. Adaptable by the _directly-involved_ — teachers, students,
parents, employers, cohort hosts — within their existing constraints.

Forkability is not a side feature. It is the operative form of the Open
Education commitment.

### 2. Feedback is bidirectional

The course expects, and is shaped by, feedback from the communities using it.
Not "user testing" in the product-design sense — _peer review_ in the
practitioner-and-scholar sense. Cohort hosts and partner organizations are
co-authors of the curriculum's next state.

### 3. Co-authorship is acknowledged at the level of authority, not credits

The author is _one_ of the people who made this. The principles were refined in
classrooms; the framings were sharpened in conversation; the platform was built
by a small team. _"Evan is the scribe of a thing that's been written by many
hands over many years."_ The curriculum claims authority _as a synthesis_, not
as an original.

When you, as a partner, contribute — the way the curriculum says "yes, that's
right" or "yes, that's clearer" is by **incorporating the contribution into the
next iteration**, not by issuing credit lines. This is uncomfortable in
conventional publication economies; it's honest in pedagogical-practice
economies.

## What this curriculum brings to your community

Concretely:

### A vocabulary

The course's vocabulary — Vibetoading and Frogramming, twinning, the five
strands, PBIS, the spiderweb-and-spiral, the JEJ chain, the mastery contract,
fluid roles — is designed to **support the kinds of conversations communities
need to have about programming education**. If your community already has its
own vocabulary, the course's terms can plug in alongside, or replace, or refine
yours — whatever your context needs.

The vocabulary's job is to **name implicit things explicitly** so that
conversations across role-boundaries (learner ↔ teacher ↔ companion ↔
accounta-buddy) can happen without one party feeling the other already knows
what they're trying to learn.

### A set of design principles you can hold up to your context

The design principles in `pedagogy.md` § "Design principles" are _named_ so that
they can be evaluated, adapted, or rejected. Open Education, Learner Trust comes
first, Context is Content, Process Over Product, Explicitly Teach the Implicit,
Full Complexity Max Simplicity, Connections are Concepts as method, Wear Hats
not Titles, Fluid Roles, Greg Wilson's rules, the Reusability Paradox, the
Transfer Paradox — these are the principles the curriculum operates by. If
they're not right for your context, they're at least _named_, so you can know
what you're departing from.

### An infrastructure that travels with you

The JEJ chain (JEJ → NM → embody → lenses → orchestrator) is **reusable
infrastructure** — domain-agnostic, available to any curriculum that wants the
lens system. The course is opinionated content sitting on top of this
infrastructure. **The infrastructure travels.** If your community wants to build
a different curriculum on the same lens system, it can. The Reusability Paradox
is preserved at the right level.

### A stance on intellectual agency

The course is, openly, a manifesto for human intellect and human practice in an
era where generative AI changes the landscape of who makes what. It assumes the
people in the room — your learners, your teachers, your community — are capable
of intellectual practice that machines cannot do _for_ them. It builds
intellectual confidence rather than requiring it.

This stance might fit your community's context. It might not. Where it fits, it
offers your community both a framework and a quietly political claim: **the
people you're educating are not falling behind in the AI race; they are gaining
capacities the AI cannot have.**

## The technical name for what we're doing together

There's a precise framework that names the relationship between a curriculum and
the communities that use it. It's worth knowing — not because you need to
memorize the vocabulary, but because it helps locate what's happening when we
coordinate.

The framework is **Translational Computing Education Research (TCER)** — Cole,
Malaise, & Signer (2023). It names two divides that limit progress in computing
education:

1. **Research and practice don't talk enough.** Researchers produce findings
   about how people learn to program; practitioners build curricula from
   experience and intuition; the findings rarely reach the curricula, and the
   curricula rarely inform the research.
2. **Theory and design don't talk enough.** Theoretical work (what _should_
   work) and design work (what _does_ work) develop in parallel without enough
   feedback between them.

The curriculum lives in the **trading zones** where these divides meet. Trading
zones (a term from physicist Peter Galison, applied to CER by TCER) are places
where people from different traditions coordinate locally on specific problems
without needing to agree globally on methodology or philosophy.

**Partner cohorts are trading zones in this technical sense — not user-tests.**
When a cohort runs, the cohort host's experience is itself a research
contribution. The curriculum's next iteration reflects what the cohort surfaced.
Same applies to mentors offering feedback, to friends pushing back on framings,
to colleagues whose papers shape the next revision. **Co-development IS
translational research, in the framework's technical sense.**

This is what "the course is made in community, not just for community" means
once you have the right vocabulary for it. It's not aspirational language; it's
an operational claim about how translational research actually works.

### Why this matters to you

A few practical implications:

- **Your community's experience is data, not user-testing.** It's not feedback
  we collect at the end of a delivery. It's part of how the curriculum gets
  refined, alongside (not after) its making.
- **The curriculum and its infrastructure (`lenses/embody`) are themselves
  coordinated.** Two translational research artifacts in the same trading zone,
  each shaping the other's next iteration. If your community's experience
  surfaces something the infrastructure doesn't yet show, that travels upstream
  to the infrastructure side, not just to the curriculum side.
- **Reflexive analysis is welcome — including reflexive critique of this
  framework.** Questioning whether TCER's framing matches your community's
  reality is itself doing translational research. We'd rather hear "the
  trading-zone framing doesn't fit our context for these reasons" than have you
  nod politely and quietly adapt around it.
- **The two traps TCER names — _the translational imperative_ (pressuring all
  work to justify broader impact) and the _pipeline misconception_ (translation
  is cyclical, not linear) — both apply to community work too.** Some
  adaptations don't need to scale. Some feedback loops are slow. The cyclical
  shape is honest.

For the deeper analysis, see `translational-framing.md`. For the embody-side
companion, see `DGMD-E-1-artifacts/embodying-tcer/`.

The framework is the user's own (Cole-Malaise-Signer 2023). Yoshi Malaise and
Beat Signer also co-authored the Explorotron paper that the `lenses/embody`
infrastructure implements. _Authority is plural and wide_ — the discourse
community that shaped this curriculum is itself a trading zone the curriculum
operates inside.

## What we ask of you

A small set of asks, in the spirit of _Open Education_ not as licensing but as
relationship:

1. **Tell us what you needed to change.** When you adapt the course for your
   community, let us know what didn't fit. Even — especially — if you don't have
   time to contribute the change back upstream. The _fact_ that you had to
   change something is information the curriculum needs.

2. **Tell us what worked unexpectedly well.** Adaptations that worked
   surprisingly well are the most valuable feedback. They reveal what the
   original commitments were really doing.

3. **Tell us what surprised you.** Especially in cohorts where the learners'
   lived experience and background was different from what the curriculum's
   defaults assume. _Whatever your learners bring is an asset already in the
   room_ is the operative claim — but it's a claim that needs continuous testing
   in new contexts.

4. **Keep your adaptations as open as you can.** If your community can manage
   it, fork publicly. Let other communities find your adaptations. The
   curriculum-as-Quine ethos works only if the network of forks is itself open
   enough to be reachable.

5. **Pass the practice on.** When your cohort finishes, the cohort members
   become _companions_, _teachers_, _accounta-buddies_ for their own next
   learners. The curriculum was designed assuming this. Honor it.

## What's tricky about this register

Three honest things.

### Community work is slow

Curriculum that's truly being made in community moves at the pace of the
communities. Cohorts cycle. Conversations take time. Feedback loops are long. If
you're partnering with this curriculum, _expect the pace to be patient._ Things
will shift in the next iteration, not the current one. That's a feature.

### Communities have different needs

A community of novice programmers has different constraints, different prior
knowledge, different access patterns, different priorities than a community of
mid-career professionals retraining in Belgium, than a community of high-school
CS students in the US, than a community of design-school students who've never
written code. **The curriculum's default settings are not universal.** When you
partner, expect to negotiate.

### Co-authorship is not always equitable in practice

Even with the best intentions, asymmetries of who-has-time, who-has-
publishing-access, who-gets-credited-where show up in any co-authored work. This
curriculum is committed to being _honest about that asymmetry_ rather than
performing flat collaboration when it isn't real. Where contributions are
uneven, we'll try to say so. Where attribution is unclear, we'll try to
investigate. Where someone's shaping work is invisible, we'll try to surface it.

If you find a place where your contribution is invisible and you'd like it
surfaced, **say so.** _"Authority is plural and wide"_ is not a slogan; it's a
practical commitment that needs your participation to operate.

## A small closing

The course exists because people taught the author how to teach. The people who
taught included alumni who didn't know they were teaching, colleagues who pushed
back over coffee, students whose questions reshaped a chapter, and writers whose
work shaped the framing across fifteen years.

When your community uses this curriculum — or adapts it — or critiques it — or
contributes to it — you join that long conversation. There's no membership card.
There's just the work, carefully scribed, passed hand to hand.

> _"Never teach alone."_ (Greg Wilson, Rule 5)

We meant that as more than a rule for individuals. We meant it as the shape of
the whole curriculum.

Welcome.
